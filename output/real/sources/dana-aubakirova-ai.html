    Next Thursday, November 12, the SAIR Foundation is co-hosting the Physical AI Summit 2026 with Brown University in Providence.

    I have a personal stake in this one. I serve on Brown's external AI advisory board, and bringing SAIR to campus has been on my mind since the day I joined. Brenda Rubenstein and I will open the morning together, and I am grateful to her and to the Brown team for making this happen.

    The theme is physical AI: intelligence that leaves the server and enters the world through robots, sensors, materials, and the systems we live alongside. Brown is an unusually good place to ask what that means. George Karniadakis has spent decades building the mathematics that lets machine learning respect physical law, and George Konidaris works on how robots learn to act in the open world. Both are giving keynotes.

    A panel moderated by our SAIR colleague Milo Chamberlin brings Srinath Sridhar, John Sous, Puneet Dokania and Miguel Bessa together on how embodied intelligence moves from lab to deployment.

    The afternoon turns to people. Suresh Venkatasubramanian leads a conversation on human-computer and human-robot interaction with Sydney Skybetter, whose work is literally choreography for robots, alongside Dana Aubakirova of Hugging Face, Diana Freed and Daphna Buchsbaum. The question I keep coming back to is not what machines can do, but how we will live with them.

    SAIR co-founder and Nobel Laureate Barry Barish will deliver a keynote after lunch. We also have two firesides: Di Jin of Nebius with me in the morning, and Eliot Horowitz of MongoDB with Viet Andy N. of Nebius, both proud Brown Alumni in the afternoon.

    The day closes with two panels on the institutions that will carry scientific AI forward. Tejal Desai, Dean of Brown Engineering, moderates a discussion on the university's role with David Parkes of Harvard SEAS, Brenda Rubenstein, Garud Iyengar of Columbia, and Peter Chin of Dartmouth. Then Elke Trilla of DLA Piper leads Scott Clark of Talaria, Asher Labovich of Liquid, Jacob Fleisig of Snorkel, and Bob Chatham of the Slater Fund on industry's part in scaling this work beyond the lab.

    None of this happens without our sponsors. Thank you to Susquehanna International Group, Nebius, Snorkel AI, Liquid AI, DLA Piper, and Talaria Scientific for backing the day. Universities, industry, builders, and capital, in one room, on one problem. That is what SAIR exists to do.

    𝙃𝙤𝙬 𝙙𝙤 𝙮𝙤𝙪 𝙛𝙞𝙣𝙙 𝘿𝙉𝘼 𝙞𝙣 𝙩𝙧𝙞𝙡𝙡𝙞𝙤𝙣𝙨 𝙤𝙛 𝙩𝙤𝙠𝙚𝙣𝙨 𝙤𝙛 𝙬𝙚𝙗 𝙩𝙚𝙭𝙩? 🧬

    Sharing the insights and lessons learned below ⬇️

    That was one of the data questions behind Carbon-X. Our answer is FineDNA, the 300B-token mix we trained it on, now open on the Hugging Face Hub.

    15% of FineDNA is biology text where DNA sits inside the writing: papers, protocols, forum posts. It is what links the two "languages" the model learns, raw genomes and English, and it is rare. We searched FineWeb-Edu, FinePDFs, PubMed Central, bioRxiv and Biostars, and kept about 3B tokens of the densest part. Here is how, in two steps.

    𝙎𝙩𝙚𝙥 1: 𝙛𝙞𝙣𝙙 𝙥𝙖𝙜𝙚𝙨 𝙩𝙝𝙖𝙩 𝙖𝙘𝙩𝙪𝙖𝙡𝙡𝙮 𝙘𝙤𝙣𝙩𝙖𝙞𝙣 𝘿𝙉𝘼.
    We kept a page if it had:
    → 15+ nucleotide letters in a row
    → at least 3 different letters (so AAAAAAAAAAAAAAA doesn't count)
    → a biology keyword (gene, primer, PCR…)
    Matches inside {curly braces} are skipped, because in scraped text they are usually LaTeX.

    We also tried the usual approach, a classifier that scores how "biology-like" a page is. In earlier ablations on a smaller Qwen model, with the same English data and budget, pages with DNA beat pages about general genomics research on both DNA fundamentals and lab sequence tasks.
    𝙇𝙚𝙨𝙨𝙤𝙣 1: 𝙛𝙞𝙡𝙩𝙚𝙧 𝙛𝙤𝙧 𝙬𝙝𝙖𝙩 𝙮𝙤𝙪 𝙬𝙖𝙣𝙩 𝙩𝙝𝙚 𝙢𝙤𝙙𝙚𝙡 𝙩𝙤 𝙧𝙚𝙖𝙙, 𝙣𝙤𝙩 𝙛𝙤𝙧 𝙩𝙝𝙚 𝙩𝙤𝙥𝙞𝙘.

    𝙎𝙩𝙚𝙥 2: 𝙡𝙤𝙤𝙠 𝙖𝙩 𝙬𝙝𝙖𝙩 𝙮𝙤𝙪 𝙛𝙤𝙪𝙣𝙙.
    Most DNA on the web is short. The median sequence is about 20 bases, the length of a PCR primer. We cut at the 90th percentile, 39 bases. Pages with a longer sequence form the "p90" subset: 137k pages, 3B tokens.
    𝙇𝙚𝙨𝙨𝙤𝙣 2: 𝙡𝙤𝙤𝙠 𝙖𝙩 𝙩𝙝𝙚 𝙙𝙞𝙨𝙩𝙧𝙞𝙗𝙪𝙩𝙞𝙤𝙣 𝙤𝙛 𝙮𝙤𝙪𝙧 𝙨𝙞𝙜𝙣𝙖𝙡 𝙗𝙚𝙛𝙤𝙧𝙚 𝙮𝙤𝙪 𝙥𝙞𝙘𝙠 𝙖 𝙩𝙝𝙧𝙚𝙨𝙝𝙤𝙡𝙙.

    Then we kept the shorter pages too, but upsampled the long-DNA ones to half of the biology text. That did at least as well as using the data as found.
    𝙇𝙚𝙨𝙨𝙤𝙣 3: 𝙪𝙥𝙨𝙖𝙢𝙥𝙡𝙚 𝙩𝙝𝙚 𝙙𝙚𝙣𝙨𝙚 𝙥𝙖𝙧𝙩; 𝙙𝙤𝙣'𝙩 𝙩𝙝𝙧𝙤𝙬 𝙩𝙝𝙚 𝙧𝙚𝙨𝙩 𝙖𝙬𝙖𝙮.

    The web had one more gap: long genomic sequences almost never come with prose around them.

    So we wrote some. Gemini 3 models described annotated genes, with sequences of up to 25 kb right next to the text. Half the documents are in fill-in-the-middle format.

    That's the recipe in the final Carbon-X mix: p90 gets 7% of training tokens, as much as all other mined biology text, so it's read ~7 times. The Gemini descriptions add 1% more.

    None of these lessons is specific to biology. If you are building data for code, chemistry or math, the same questions apply: what does the model actually need to read, how much of it exists, and how much weight should it get?

            1w

            When I started working on Carbon X, I could follow conversations about promoters and splice sites. But put an actual DNA sequence in front of me, and I didn’t know where to begin.

            LLMs can face a similar gap: learning about biology from text doesn’t automatically teach them to interpret DNA.

            With Carbon-X, we explored connecting the two by training a language model on both text and DNA.

            It left me wondering: could a similar recipe work for other modalities?
            Seismic signals and geological reports. Sensor readings and engineering manuals.
            Text captures what we’ve learned to describe. The underlying data may contain patterns we haven’t learned to explain.

            Could connecting the two help us discover what we’re missing?
            I don’t know how far this recipe can generalize, but Carbon-X offers some clues. We shared what worked and what didn’t. More in the blog post. 🧬

            While I was writing this, my AI assistant got cut off by a bio safety filter. It was summarizing my own team's notes on the model we released today 😅

            Frontier labs are moving fast into biology but the scientists and clinicians who'd get the most out of these models are unfortunately the ones running into walls like this one (and every day).

            So we partnered with TIGEM (Telethon Institute of Genetics and Medicine), Naple "Federico II" University and Zhongguancun Academy to train *Carbon-X*

            Carbon-X is an open 14B model that reads raw DNA and talks about it in plain English. On our DNA benchmarks it scores 67%, vs 61% for Claude Opus 5.5 and 50% for Gemini 4 Argon, and its within a point of GPT-6.1 Sol at about 1000x lower cost.

            We kept human and animal viruses out of the training data. The weights are open and the model runs on hardware accessible to academia (a laptop).

            One example from the blog: give frontier models an 8kb stretch of the NF1 gene with a mutation at a splice site. Most of them call it benign, refuse, or run out of tokens. Carbon-X says pathogenic, because the mutation breaks the splice site.

            This century, biology may be where AI matters most. We want to get there with the safety work done in the open and every lab in the world able to build on it. Go try it, and tell us what you find.
