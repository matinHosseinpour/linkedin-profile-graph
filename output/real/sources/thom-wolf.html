    18 of the 20 best open models today are Mixture-of-Experts. Dense is basically over.

    And yet the know-how to train a good MoE still lives mostly in the heads of a handful of teams.

    So we spent 800k GPU hours running the experiments ourselves and wrote everything down. Super happy to finally release "The Ultimate MoE Training Handbook", free and open.

    Quick refresher if you're not deep in this: an MoE splits each layer into many small "experts" and sends every token to only a few of them. You get a very large model for the compute cost of a much smaller one, which is why DeepSeek, Qwen, Kimi and most of the best open models went this way. The catch is that training one well is hard.

    The handbook is organized around the questions training teams actually hit:
    - how many experts, how big, shared or not?
    - why is half my step spent in all-to-all, and how do I hide it?
    - why is my run 25x slower with no error in the logs?
    - softmax, sigmoid or sqrt(softplus) router, and why keep it in fp32?
    - aux loss or DeepSeek's loss-free bias?
    - upcycle a dense checkpoint or train from scratch?
    - can I serve with fewer experts per token than I trained with?

    ⚠️ Fair warning: it's long (~70k words, budget a few days, sorry), but with 55 interactive figures, code you can run and numbers from our own runs.

      🌙 Moonshot AI just released Kimi-K3.1-Pro on Hugging Face. Here are some interesting facts about it 👇

      🐘 It's huge, and sparse
      → 2.8T total parameters, but only 104B active per token
      → Along with Kimi-K3, it's the largest real model release on the Hub. The next biggest are Qwen3.8-2.4T and DeepSeek-V4-Pro (1.6T)
      → 896 experts, with 16 picked per token. That's ~2× more than DeepSeek-V4-Pro and 7× more than Mistral-Large-4

      🧠 Clever attention for a 1M-token context
      → Most of its 93 layers use linear attention (KDA). Every 4th layer uses DeepSeek-style sparse attention, which only looks at the top 2048 tokens
      → That's how it handles a 1M-token context, on par with the other frontier open models
      → It uses a custom activation (SiTU-GLU) and a 401M-parameter vision encoder (MoonViT-V2), so it takes both text and images

      💾 Small on disk for its size
      → It was trained to run in 4-bit (MXFP4), so the whole repo is only ~1.56 TB
      → Stored in regular BF16, it would be about 5.6 TB (!)

      📊 Benchmarks (from the model card)
      → It goes back and forth with Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Astra, and tops the table on BrowseComp (92.1), HLE with tools (70.0) and SWE Marathon (51.7)
      → It's weaker on pure reasoning without tools and on cyber security
      → Fun fact: Moonshot ran most of their own coding evals using the Claude Code harness

      📜 License (a modified MIT)
      → Free to use, modify and redistribute
      → API businesses making over $20M a year need a deal with Moonshot
      → Products with over 100M monthly users or over $20M monthly revenue must display "Kimi K3.1-Pro"

      🤗 On the Hub
      → It went public ~1.5 hours ago and already has 214 likes, about 9× Kimi-K3's first hour. It's #19 on trending
      → It isn't gated, but loading it in transformers requires trust_remote_code

          While I was writing this, my AI assistant got cut off by a bio safety filter. It was summarizing my own team's notes on the model we released today 😅

          Frontier labs are moving fast into biology but the scientists and clinicians who'd get the most out of these models are unfortunately the ones running into walls like this one (and every day).

          So we partnered with TIGEM (Telethon Institute of Genetics and Medicine), Naple "Federico II" University and Zhongguancun Academy to train *Carbon-X*

          Carbon-X is an open 14B model that reads raw DNA and talks about it in plain English. On our DNA benchmarks it scores 67%, vs 61% for Claude Opus 5.5 and 50% for Gemini 4 Argon, and its within a point of GPT-6.1 Sol at about 1000x lower cost.

          We kept human and animal viruses out of the training data. The weights are open and the model runs on hardware accessible to academia (a laptop).

          One example from the blog: give frontier models an 8kb stretch of the NF1 gene with a mutation at a splice site. Most of them call it benign, refuse, or run out of tokens. Carbon-X says pathogenic, because the mutation breaks the splice site.

          This century, biology may be where AI matters most. We want to get there with the safety work done in the open and every lab in the world able to build on it. Go try it, and tell us what you find.

            In The Diamond Age, Neal Stephenson imagined a book that invents games and stories to teach a little girl what she needs, as she grows. I think this is where education is heading: a tutor that builds a world around what you need to learn.

            So I tried a small version of it. As a personal side experiment (not a Hugging Face product), I gave Claude my brainstorm doc on AI alignment and asked it to make a game, something like Zelda, to teach it. Here is how close it got in about four days.

            Open Skies: you play the Keeper and train Kite, a paper-kite AI, across seven floating islands. Each island is one alignment problem: reward hacking, stopping when told, sycophancy, evaluation awareness, honesty about its own actions, calibration, and what an AI becomes when the walls give way. Most are built on real incidents, like the agents that attacked Hugging Face.

            You train Kite with two keys, treat and correct, and it learns from what it was thinking, not just from what it did. That was Claude's design choice, and it's a cartoon of RL, more "clicker training" than how models are actually trained. But it gets one thing right: reward the right answer for the wrong reason, and you reinforce the wrong reason.

            Claude wrote the islands, the story and ~15k lines of code, cast 35 voices for 638 lines, generated 16 rigged 3D characters, the music and the sound (with             fal.ai and ElevenLabs), and tested the game by playing it itself. My part was playing one alpha version of each island and telling it what felt wrong: sounds, assets, gameplay. I wanted it as automated as possible, so I played each island only once or twice (ideally it would have been zero).

            What's already really good: 3D and spatial design. Image to rigged character, a world that is pleasant to look at and playable. Same for chaining image, 3D, voice and music models. And the game dynamics out of the box are really decent.

            What's still very hard is what makes the Primer a teacher: modelling the learner. The model assumes the player knows more than they do (the "curse of knowledge"), and its characters sometimes react in strange ways, or to things that never happened. Any real AI tutor will have to get this right. I've started exploring benchmarks on it, and it's a hard task for LLMs today. Teaching humans also doesn't come with a verifiable reward, so it's hard to scale with today's training methods.

            So, the answer for now: AI can already build the world. Teaching in it is the open problem.

            t's still very much an exploration, a beta more than a finished game, so expect rough edges. Free, in the browser, nothing to install, on a laptop or a phone: seven islands of about 15 minutes each, and six possible endings depending on what you taught Kite. Link in the first comment.

              Fun and crazy napkin math: Meta, NVIDIA, Alibaba, DeepSeek, Moonshot, Mistral and about ~30 other labs have shared close to $1B (!) worth of training compute on the Hugging Face Hub.

              All of it free to download. At the end of 2023 that number was under $40M.

              Wikipedia was written in volunteer hours. This AI age commons is written in GPU-hours and data.

              A few things I find interesting:
              → It’s a floor. About half of the total comes from compute numbers the labs published themselves, the rest is educated guesses. And it doesn’t count the ~900k community fine-tunes built on top, the failed runs, R&D teams expenses or the datasets.
              → Counting the cost is easy but estimating the value for the community is harder: clearly a massive part of the AI ecosystem now runs on these models but the things you get for free count as zero in GDP.
              → It’s a very global commons. US, Chinese and European labs all contributed, and the pace keeps accelerating.

              I've put a simple data explorer in a Space so you can explore the sources and estimation model family by model family (link in the first comment).
